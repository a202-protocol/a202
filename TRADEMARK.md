## Nominative use

Using the name to refer to the thing it names is ordinary and is not restricted. That covers:

- naming the specification, a version of it, a proposal, a reason code, or a schema identifier, in documentation, code, articles, talks, or academic work;
- describing an implementation by its relationship to the specification, in the form "conforms to A202 v0.1", "implements A202", or "compatible with A202", where the claim is accurate;
- the identifiers the specification itself defines and an implementation has to reproduce in order to interoperate: the `A202-` reason-code prefix, the `A202-NNNN` proposal identifiers, the `a202-commercial/0.1` specification version string, the `a202-scope/` role scope identifiers, and the schema `$id` values.

The condition on all of these is accuracy. A claim of conformance that the implementation does not meet is a false claim about the specification as well as about the implementation.

## What conformance is claimed through

A conformance claim has a defined mechanism, and it is the conformance grade in [conformance/conformance-grades-v0.1.md](conformance/conformance-grades-v0.1.md). Three points from that document bear on how the name may be used alongside a claim.

1. A grade is reported from an assessment, at the level it was assessed at, and is never inferred at a higher one. An unassessed dimension reads as unassessed.
2. Running the published conformance suite is something anyone may do, and a self-run result is a legitimate statement by the party that ran it. It is not a grade, because a published fixture can be hard-coded and a self-run result carries no independent evidence that it was not.
3. No external body recognises a grade. It is a statement by whoever issued it, about one implementation, scoped to one specification version, and no material may describe it as certification by a standards body, a regulator, an auditor, or an insurer.

So "passes the published A202 conformance suite" is a statement a party may make about its own run. "A202 certified" is not a statement this project's machinery can support, from anyone.

## What needs written permission

Permission in writing is needed before:

- using A202 as, or inside, the name of a product, service, company, organisation, or domain, whether alone or joined to another word;
- using it in a logo, a wordmark, or any styling that presents it as the brand of something other than this specification;
- using it in a way that suggests this project or its sponsor endorses, sponsors, certifies, or is affiliated with a product, an organisation, or a body;
- using it so that a reader could take a modified or derived specification for this one. A fork is free to exist and free to say accurately what it derives from, and calling itself A202 would misdescribe which document a counterparty is relying on.

The last is the reason the mark is held at all. A counterparty acting on an A202 record needs the name to identify one specification with one set of rules, and a name that identifies several identifies none.
